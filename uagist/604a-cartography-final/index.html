GIST 604A – Cartography Final

Simplified Bedrock Geology of Adirondack Park, New York

Project Overview

For my GIST 604A Cartography final, I created a simplified bedrock geologic map of Adirondack Park in northern New York. I wanted to make a map that still showed the major geologic patterns of the Adirondacks without trying to display every unit included in the original New York State bedrock geology data.

The original geology is extremely detailed, which works well at larger scales but becomes difficult to read when the entire Adirondack Park is shown on one map. A large part of this project was figuring out how much of that detail I could simplify while still keeping the geology meaningful.

The final map combines the generalized bedrock geology with water bodies, selected towns, shaded relief, the Adirondack Park boundary, and a locator map showing the park within New York State. I used the project to apply several concepts from the course, including generalization, qualitative color, visual hierarchy, labeling, typography, shaded relief, and inset map design.

Workflow

I built the map in ArcGIS Pro using several geology, hydrography, elevation, and reference datasets. One of the first challenges was that Adirondack Park crosses two New York State bedrock geology sheets, so I had to combine the Adirondack and Hudson–Mohawk datasets before I could work with the geology as one continuous layer.

  1. Clipped the Adirondack and Hudson–Mohawk bedrock geology datasets to the Adirondack Park boundary.
  2. Merged the clipped geology into one working bedrock dataset.
  3. Used the New York State bedrock geology master legend to interpret the original geologic unit codes.
  4. Created new fields for broad geologic age, generalized rock type, and the final map class.
  5. Grouped the original detailed units into broader age and lithologic categories.
  6. Used Pairwise Dissolve to remove boundaries between neighboring polygons that had been assigned to the same generalized class.
  7. Added water bodies and selected towns to provide geographic reference without adding too much clutter.
  8. Converted USGS GNIS populated-place coordinates into point features using XY Table To Point.
  9. Mosaicked USGS DEM tiles into a continuous elevation raster covering Adirondack Park.
  10. Created a hillshade from the DEM and clipped it to the park boundary using Extract by Mask.
  11. Symbolized the generalized geology with Unique Values using a muted qualitative color palette.
  12. Created a New York State locator map showing where Adirondack Park is located within the state.
  13. Built the final 11 × 17 landscape layout with the map, legend, town labels, scale bars, north arrow, locator map, projection information, and data credits.

Original Bedrock Geology Sheets

Adirondack Park overlaps portions of two New York State bedrock geology sheets: the Adirondack sheet and the Hudson–Mohawk sheet. Looking at the original maps makes the main challenge of this project pretty obvious: there is a huge amount of geologic detail.

Trying to carry all of those individual units into a single map of the entire park would have created an enormous legend and made many of the colors and boundaries difficult to distinguish. I used these maps as the starting point, then simplified the geology into a much smaller set of classes that made more sense at the scale of my final map.

Geologic Unit Generalization

Generalizing the geology was probably the biggest part of the project. The original data contains dozens of individual bedrock units, and many of them are much more detailed than I needed for a map covering all of Adirondack Park.

I decided to group the units using two things that would still be useful to someone reading the map: geologic age and broad rock type. This let me reduce the number of classes substantially without losing the major geologic patterns I wanted the map to show.

Generalization Fields

I added three fields to help organize the original units:

  • AGE_GROUP – the broad geologic age of the unit.
  • ROCK_GROUP – a simplified lithologic grouping.
  • MAP_CLASS – the final age-and-rock-type class used to symbolize the map.

Final Geologic Classes

  • Cenozoic – Quaternary deposits
  • Mesozoic – Intrusive igneous rocks
  • Paleozoic – Carbonate sedimentary rocks
  • Paleozoic – Mixed sedimentary rocks
  • Paleozoic – Siliciclastic sedimentary rocks
  • Precambrian – Anorthosite and related mafic rocks
  • Precambrian – Granitic, syenitic and charnockitic gneiss
  • Precambrian – Mafic metamorphic rocks
  • Precambrian – Marble and calc-silicate rocks
  • Precambrian – Metasedimentary gneiss and quartzite

I kept more detail within the Precambrian rocks because they make up so much of Adirondack Park and are a major part of the geology I wanted to show. The younger rocks cover much smaller areas, so I was able to group those more broadly.

Once the units were classified, I dissolved polygons with the same final map class. This removed a lot of unnecessary internal boundaries, including the seam between the original source sheets, and made the geology read much more clearly at 1:800,000.

Original Geologic Unit Reference

The source data uses abbreviated unit codes, so I used the New York State bedrock geology master legend to identify what each unit represented and decide which age and rock group it belonged in.

Cartographic Design

For the geology, I wanted colors that were different enough to separate the units but still felt like they belonged together. I ended up using a muted palette instead of very bright categorical colors. I also tried to give related age groups some visual similarity while still making the individual rock types easy to tell apart.

Water is shown in blue, and I used a stronger blue outline for the Adirondack Park boundary. This also fits the park well because its boundary is commonly referred to as the Blue Line.

I created the hillshade from USGS elevation data rather than using a basemap hillshade. I kept it fairly subtle so the terrain would help show the shape of the Adirondacks without overpowering the geology.

I also intentionally left out a lot of reference features. I only labeled a small number of towns and kept the hydrography fairly simple. Adding everything available in the source data made the map feel crowded very quickly, so deciding what not to show became just as important as deciding what to include.

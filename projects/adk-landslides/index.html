Adirondack Landslide Monitoring with Multispectral Imagery

Project Overview

This is an ongoing, multi-phase project to map and monitor landslides in the eastern High Peaks region of the Adirondacks using satellite imagery, NAIP aerial photos, and terrain data. Right now I’m in the early “trial” phase: testing classification approaches, learning where they fail, and building the infrastructure for a long-term monitoring system.

The current workflow focuses on distinguishing newly exposed bare rock from forest and water to identify landslides that occurred between 2018 and 2024. Longer-term, the goal is to integrate a wider range of satellite sources and near-real-time imagery so that landslides can be detected and mapped soon after they occur, especially in remote parts of the Adirondacks where new slides often go unnoticed.

Study Area & Data

The study area covers the eastern High Peaks region of the Adirondacks in Upstate New York, including well-known mass-wasting slopes around the Great Range and adjacent valleys.

The current phase uses:

  • NAIP 1-m aerial imagery (2017, 2019, 2022) for high-resolution context and validation.
  • Landsat-based multispectral embeddings for annual land-cover classification from 2018–2024.
  • Sentinel-2 RGB imagery (10 m) to visualize recent years, including the 2025 landslide season.
  • Digital elevation models and derived slope/hillshade to help separate steep rock faces from flatter human-built surfaces.

For this class project, land-cover classes were intentionally kept simple: Forest, Water, and Bare Rock. Newly exposed bare rock between time steps is interpreted as potential landslide area, which is then refined using elevation and slope masks.

Methods & Workflow

The workflow is built around yearly land-cover classifications and change detection:

  • Use embeddings-based land-cover classification to generate annual maps (2018–2024) with classes: forest, water, bare rock.
  • Apply terrain and built-area masks to reduce obvious false positives from infrastructure and low-slope surfaces that still share a “bare” spectral signature.
  • Compare consecutive years to flag pixels that transition from forest to bare rock. Those pixels are grouped into polygons and filtered by minimum area to represent individual landslides.
  • Use NAIP and Sentinel-2 imagery for manual validation and for refining the polygon boundaries in the most complex areas.

The project has already gone through several rounds of trial and error. One of the biggest issues is that landslide scars and high-elevation summit bedrock often have nearly identical spectral signatures. Without extra context, the classifier happily labels both as “new bare rock,” which inflates landslide counts in areas that have actually been exposed for decades.

Another challenge is separating human-made surfaces (roads, parking lots, talus at ski areas, etc.) from true mass-wasting features. Many of these features sit on moderate slopes and are spectrally similar to rockfalls and debris fans, so additional masking using DEM-derived slope, curvature, and external building/road datasets will be important in the next phase.

Limitations & Next Steps

The current maps successfully capture many large, obvious landslides but still struggle in a few consistent ways:

  • Summit bare rock vs landslide scars. Alpine bedrock and long-exposed slides share the same spectral signature as fresh failures. Without explicit summit masks or age information, those surfaces are repeatedly flagged as “new.”
  • Sparsely vegetated slopes. Some areas with thin soils or open canopy that have changed very little since 2018 still classify as “new landslides,” especially on bright, south-facing slopes.
  • Human-built surfaces. Roads, clearings, and infrastructure sometimes pass the terrain filters and show up as false positives.

Near-term work will focus on:

  • Using DEM-derived masks (elevation bands, slope, curvature) to remove summit plateaus and low-slope built areas.
  • Bringing in building/road layers to explicitly mask human infrastructure.
  • Expanding the class set beyond forest / water / bare rock to include shrub/early regrowth, talus, and alpine meadow, which should help track revegetation and reduce confusion near slide margins.
  • Refining the rainfall–landslide analysis with storm-scale metrics and better temporal alignment between precipitation events and available imagery.
  • Extending the historic inventory as far back as possible by combining older aerial photography, NAIP, and any usable pre-2018 satellite archives to estimate landslide rates through time.

Long-term, I’d like this to function as an operational landslide-monitoring tool for the Adirondacks: regularly updated maps, alerts for newly detected scars, and a public-facing web map for hikers, land managers, and researchers.

Takeaways

Even in this early phase, the project has been useful for understanding where automated approaches to landslide mapping break down: summit bedrock, sparse vegetation, and human-built surfaces all look more “landslide-like” to the classifier than they should. Working through those problems is the main focus of the next phase, along with incorporating more frequent, higher-resolution imagery for years like 2025 that were especially active.
